Three things are covered here, and they happen at different points. The **repository policy** is written while the repository is being specified, and maintained for as long as it runs. Being considered a **trusted repository** follows from meeting the conditions the NRP sets. **Certification** is a separate, optional addition.

## The repository policy

Every repository must have its own policy on corrections and modifications. The NRP does not impose a single uniform policy — policies are set according to the needs and practices of each user community.

What the policy must do is **explicitly delimit and precisely describe the scope of permitted changes**. This follows from what makes a record citable: the repository guarantees that a digital object has not been altered after it is marked as permanent, so that research referring to it remains replicable.

In particular, the policy defines:

*   **when a record is considered closed** — finalized;

*   **what changes are permissible in a finalized record.** Adding a metadata item that links to a correction or to a usage of the record is the usual example;

*   **the retention rules by record type.**

The policy should aim at **minimizing allowed changes**, preferring versioning — and for larger datasets, separately identified "patch" bundles.

> A system that allows arbitrary changes to closed records, without these guarantees, cannot be considered a repository.

The **data curator** sets the general rules for data stored in the repository, and decides on individual datasets, such as deletion requests.

The policy description can also serve as certification documentation.

## What the repository accepts

Separately from how records may change, the repository states what it takes in. This covers:

*   **the supported data formats** — entered as a list of supported formats as a minimum.  
    This can be "any";

*   **the conditions for depositing**, such as a maximum size.

Limits that come from the platform rather than from the repository are in **Limits and standard implementations**.

## Trusted repositories

The NRP expects repositories to meet the technical and organisational requirements for the infrastructure of **trusted repositories**, from the perspective of grant providers in the Czech Republic.

This is not something a repository applies for. It follows from meeting the conditions the NRP sets — and it is this status, not a certificate, that the platform works towards.

## Certification — optional

Certification is **not required** for a repository in the NRP. Some repositories pursue it on their own initiative — where a grant provider, an institution or a discipline expects it, or where the repository wants to demonstrate the standard it holds itself to. Where a repository does, it is an addition to being trusted, not a different route to it.

Several certifications exist for research data repositories. The one most often referred to in the NRP context is **CoreTrustSeal**; its requirements overlap with the FAIR principles — it does not assess only the technical properties of data, but the stewardship, sustainability and trustworthiness of the repository holding them.

### What the NRP provides for it

A repository applying for certification has to document more than its own work. The infrastructure it runs on — storage, containers, the environment the repository operates in — is provided by the NRP, and the repository cannot describe it from its own records.

Reports on the state of the environment are being prepared to cover this. They are intended as material a repository can use when applying.
